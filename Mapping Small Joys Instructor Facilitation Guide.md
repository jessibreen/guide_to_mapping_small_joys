# Mapping Small Joys: Instructor Facilitation Guide

## Overview

The map matters less than the conversation it starts. Students mark the places on campus where small things make them happy, then compare maps and find out how differently people live the same campus. The facilitator's role is to steer that conversation from the easy, personal stuff toward harder questions about bodies, identity, belonging and representation.

**What students do**

- Read the Small Joys zine, which introduces what a small joy can be.
- Mark their own small joys on a printed campus map, using any symbols, colors, words or drawings they like.
- Share their maps, notice overlaps and gaps, and discuss what those differences reveal.

**Why it works**

- Joy is an easy way in. Everyone has something to say about it, and there are no wrong answers.
- A map puts experience on paper where it can be compared. Differences that would stay vague in conversation are right there on the page.
- Once people talk about where they feel good, the places where they don't tend to come up on their own. That gets you to identity, access and belonging without asking anyone to disclose anything.

**Learning goals**

1. Recognize that a place is experienced, not just located.
2. Notice that the same space can feel different depending on body, identity, schedule and history.
3. Connect personal experience on campus to wider questions about who maps are made for and whose experiences they leave out.

## Before class

Plan for about 75 minutes, with at least half the time reserved for discussion. If you only have 50 minutes, shorten the mapping, not the talking.

| Segment | Time (min) | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome and zine | 10 | Introduce the exercise; students read or flip through the zine |
| Mapping | 20 | Students mark their small joys on the campus map |
| Pair or small-group share | 10 | Students explain their maps to one or two peers |
| Whole-group discussion | 30 | The guided sequence in this document |
| Closing reflection | 5 | One-sentence takeaway, written or spoken |

**Materials**

- One printed campus map per student, ideally 11 x 17 in (tabloid) so there is room to draw. Print a few spares.
- One copy of the Small Joys zine per student.
- Drawing materials: colored pencils, markers, crayons, stickers, sticky dots, scissors, glue, scrap paper or tape. Variety matters more than quality. A messy mix of supplies tells students there's no single right way to do this.
- Wall space, tables or the floor for displaying maps side by side.
- Optional: sticky notes for the gallery walk; a blank map at the front of the room for collective marks.

**Room setup**

- Put materials in a shared pile in the center of tables, not at each seat. Reaching and sharing gets people talking early.
- Arrange seating so everyone can see each other for the discussion, such as a circle or U-shape.
- Plan where maps will be displayed so students can walk around and look.

**Prepare yourself**

- Make your own map beforehand. You'll understand the exercise better, and if you share yours first, students are more likely to open up.
- Know your campus support resources (counseling, accessibility services, identity-based centers) in case a conversation touches something heavy.
- Decide your own boundaries: what you will share about yourself, and how you will handle a comment that harms others in the room.

## Leading the mapping

Keep instructions short and open; the less you specify, the more varied and interesting the maps. While students draw, your main job is to reassure them that whatever they're doing is fine.

**Framing script** (adapt freely)

> "Today we're making maps, but not the kind that get you from A to B. We're mapping small joys: the moments and places on campus that make your day a little better. The zine gives some ideas, but what counts as joy is completely up to you. It might be a bench in the sun, a smell from the bakery, a hallway where you run into friends, or a quiet corner nobody else knows about. There is no right way to draw this. Use symbols, colors, words, doodles, whatever makes sense to you. You won't be graded on art. We'll spend most of our time talking about what you made."

**Instructions to give**

1. Read through the zine and think about your own small joys on campus.
2. Mark them on the map however you like. Use the margins or the back if a joy doesn't fit a single spot, like a walking route, a time of day or a sound.
3. Make your own key or legend if it helps, but you don't have to.
4. If you finish early, add the places that feel neutral, or that you avoid. (Offer this quietly to early finishers; don't announce it to the whole room.)

**While students map**

- Circulate and ask curious questions rather than giving suggestions: "Tell me about this one." "Why that color?" "Is this a place or a time?"
- If someone is stuck, ask about their routine: "Walk me through a normal Tuesday. Where do you breathe out?"
- If someone says they have no joys on campus, take that seriously. Let them map what is close to joy, what they wish existed, or joys just off campus. That map will be useful when you get to discussion.
- Notice different approaches as you walk around: symbols vs words, routes vs points, indoor vs outdoor, crowded vs solitary. You'll use these observations later.
- Give a five-minute and a one-minute warning. Unfinished maps are fine.

**Pair share** (10 minutes)

Before the whole group, have students explain their map to one or two neighbors. This warms everyone up and lets quieter students rehearse. Ask pairs to find one joy they share and one that surprised them.

## Leading the discussion

The discussion moves through six stages, from easy and shared toward personal and structural. Each stage builds trust for the next, so don't skip ahead. You also don't have to reach the last stage. If the room comes alive somewhere, stay there.

You don't need to ask every prompt. Pick two or three per stage, and follow students' answers more than this script.

### Stage 1: Gallery walk and first impressions (about 3 minutes)

Display all maps side by side. Ask students to walk around silently for two or three minutes, then come back together.

- What did you notice first?
- What surprised you about someone else's map?
- Did anyone map something you'd never thought of as a joy?
- Did anyone draw it in a way you wouldn't have?

Pay attention to comments about style (words or pictures, routes or dots) and about content (food, nature, people, quiet). You'll come back to both later.

### Stage 2: Shared joys (about 4 minutes)

Start with common ground. It builds a sense of a shared campus before you explore differences.

- Which places showed up on many maps? Why do you think so many people find joy there?
- What kinds of joy are common: people, food, nature, solitude, movement, routine?
- Are there joys that aren't places at all, like a time of day, a sound or a smell?

Then turn the corner: "So there's a lot we share. Now let's look at where our maps don't match."

### Stage 3: Where maps differ (about 7 minutes)

This is the hinge of the exercise. Differences are the evidence that place is experienced, not just located.

- Find a spot that's joyful on your map and absent or empty on someone else's. What might explain the difference?
- Are there places that are a joy for one person and something else (stressful, boring, uncomfortable) for another?
- Which parts of the printed map are blank on almost everyone's? Why might that be?
- Did anyone map things the printed map doesn't show at all, like a shortcut, a hidden spot or a route?

Students will often explain differences with schedules, majors, housing, jobs, friend groups or commuting. These are early signs of how circumstances shape experience. Name them gently: "So where you live and when you're here changes which campus you get."

### Stage 4: Bodies and senses (about 6 minutes)

Now shift attention to the physical, bodily experience of place. Many small joys are sensory: warmth, light, quiet, open space, the feeling of walking downhill.

- How many of your joys are about how your body feels? Temperature, light, sound, space, rest?
- What does it take for your body to get to your joyful places? Stairs, distance, hills, crowds, time?
- Are there places where you feel at ease in your body, and places where you feel watched, rushed or out of place?
- Would your map look different if you moved through campus differently, for example with a wheelchair, a stroller, a heavy bag, an injury or chronic pain?
- Do any of your joys depend on time of day? Would they feel the same at night?

Bodies are the bridge to identity. Talking about stairs or lighting is concrete and feels safe, but it gets at the same question: campus wasn't built equally for every body.

### Stage 5: Identity and belonging (about 6 minutes)

Only move here if the room feels ready. Use "some people" phrasing so nobody is asked to speak for a group or disclose anything personal.

- Who do you think campus was designed for? Where can you see that in the spaces themselves?
- Are there places on campus where some people might feel especially welcome, and others less so?
- Think about the places you avoid or never go. Is that preference, or is something keeping you out?
- Do any of your joyful places feel like they belong to a particular group? How does a space come to feel like "ours" or "theirs"?
- If you invited someone very different from you to make this map, what might they show that you didn't?

Students may bring up race, gender, disability, class, religion, language, sexuality, age, being first-generation, or being an international or commuter student. Don't push anyone to speak from their own identity. Affirm what's shared and tie it back to the maps: "So this map is also a record of where people feel they belong."

### Stage 6: Out into the wider world (about 4 minutes)

Extend the thinking beyond campus and beyond this map.

- The official campus map shows buildings and paths. What does it leave out that our maps show?
- Who makes official maps of cities, neighborhoods or countries? Whose experiences might they miss?
- Think about your hometown or neighborhood. Would a small-joys map there look like your campus one?
- If a city planner or university leader saw all of our maps together, what should they learn from them?
- What would a map of small joys miss? Whose joys might be invisible even here?

To wrap up the discussion, try something like: "Every map makes an argument about what matters. Ours say feelings and bodies matter. Knowing that, how might you read other maps differently?"

### Prompts by theme

Keep this as a quick reference when a student's comment opens a door you want to walk through.

| If a student mentions… | Try asking… |
| --- | --- |
| A place they avoid | What would have to change for that place to feel good? |
| Feeling alone or out of place | Are there others who might feel that way there too? What would help? |
| Accessibility, stairs, distance | Who else might that affect? Who decided the layout? |
| Feeling safe or unsafe | Does that change with time of day, or with who's around? |
| Cost (food, parking, events) | Which joys on campus are free? Who can't access the ones that aren't? |
| A space tied to a group or culture | How does a space come to feel like it belongs to someone? |
| Home or somewhere off campus | What does that place give you that campus doesn't? |
| A joy nobody else mapped | What does it say that only you noticed it? |

## Facilitation moves and hard moments

Nobody expects you to be an expert on identity, accessibility or geography. Mostly you'll be asking questions, keeping the room respectful and pointing people back to the maps.

**Moves that keep discussion going**

- When talk drifts into the abstract, bring it back to the paper: "Show us where that is on your map." Looking at a map instead of at each other takes some pressure off.
- Ask "How is your map different from theirs?" rather than "Why didn't you include X?"
- After a hard question, count to ten in your head before saying anything. Silence usually means people are thinking.
- Repeat back what you heard and connect it to someone else: "So Sam's joy is the empty library at 8 a.m., and Priya avoids it because it's too quiet. Same place, opposite feelings."
- Bring in new voices without putting anyone on the spot: "I'd love to hear from someone whose map we haven't talked about yet."
- Share your own map, including a place you avoid. It gives students permission to do the same.

**Ground rules to set or revisit**

- Speak from your own experience, not on behalf of a group.
- Nobody has to share anything they don't want to; passing is always allowed.
- What's personal stays in the room.
- Be curious about difference rather than correcting it.

**Handling hard moments**

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A student discloses something painful (feeling unsafe, excluded, harassed) | Thank them, acknowledge it without probing, and don't make the group analyze it. Check in privately afterward and know where to refer them. |
| A comment dismisses or stereotypes others | Address the idea, not the person: "Let's be careful about generalizing. What might that space feel like for someone else?" Follow up privately if needed. |
| One or two students dominate | "Let's hear from a map on the other side of the room." Or switch to pairs for two minutes. |
| The room goes quiet on identity questions | Step back to bodies and senses (Stage 4), which feels safer, or let students write an answer before speaking. |
| A student says campus has no joy for them | Treat it as real data. Ask the group what that tells us about campus, not what's wrong with the student. |
| Discussion turns into complaints about facilities | Validate it, then widen it: "Who decides how these spaces are built? Who gets asked?" |
| You don't know how to answer something | Say so. "I don't know; what do others think?" is a strong facilitation move. |

## Closing and adaptations

Finish where you started, with joy. If the discussion got into exclusion or discomfort, students should still leave with something hopeful and a sense that their own experience counted.

**Closing reflection** (5 minutes)

Ask each student to write or say one sentence in response to one of these:

- One thing I noticed about campus today that I hadn't before.
- One place I want to try experiencing differently.
- One question I'm still thinking about.
- One small joy I'd like to make possible for someone else.

Thank students for sharing their maps. Remind them that their maps are theirs: ask permission before displaying, photographing or keeping any of them.

**Optional follow-ups**

- A short written reflection connecting their map to a course reading on place, identity or design.
- A combined map with everyone's joys on it, displayed with permission. Ask what it shows that no single map could.
- The same exercise again at the end of term, or at night, to compare.
- A proposal from each student for one small change to a campus space that would let more people share a joy.
